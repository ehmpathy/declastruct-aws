**cells** · `provisioner × declare(revoke) × both`, walked through `grove-clone × obtain × target-only`
**feel** · **sharp**  ·  **care** · **critipath**  ·  **boundary-density** · **10 — the dense journey**

## why this is a critipath, not a bonus

`care = freq × cost` (`define.experience._.axis=care.path=criti-vs-alter`).

- **frequency** — low. a reach is revoked rarely.
- **cost** — **∞**. if revocation does not actually end the reach, there is no fallback: no
  credential to rotate, no key to expire, no secret to invalidate. the *whole* mechanism by which
  this grant is reversible is one edit to one trust policy. if that edit does not work, access
  cannot be withdrawn at all.

🔴 **and this walk earned its ∞ rating by finding exactly that.** the revoke the vision described —
*delete the statement* — is **not expressible by this repo** (`set.delete = null` on the ROLE's DAO),
and the intuitive attempt leaves the door open. an ∞-cost claim that nobody traces to code is a
guess; `[t3]` traces it.

⇒ per the care table, an **∞ cost at any nonzero frequency is a critipath**, and rarity relaxes
severity only while a fallback still reaches the goal. here no fallback exists. so a rare event with
no recovery path is critical, and its demo is owed.

## the narrative

the reach is born, lives, and dies. this is that arc, told as one continuous stretch of time rather
than four separate snapshots — because the interesting failures live between the steps, not in them.

the provisioner applies. the plan reads three `CREATE`s and everything else `KEEP`. a week later the
ahbode half merges and a clone on a grove starts to run demo suites; for a month it is boring, which
is the intended experience.

then the reason ends. maybe the grove no longer does demo work; maybe an incident makes someone want
the door shut now. the provisioner deletes the trust statement and applies.

and here is where the vision's central promise either holds or does not. there is no key to rotate.
there is no credential in a vault to invalidate, no secret to grep the fleet for. **there is one
statement, and it is rewritten to name a principal the grove is not.**

🔴 **that sentence used to read "and it is gone", and the code says the ROLE cannot be.**
`DeclaredAwsIamRoleDao` declares `set.delete = null` — as do policy, user, instance profile, and oidc
provider — and `src/**` holds no `DeleteRoleCommand` at all. so the intuitive revoke, *delete the
declaration and apply*, does **not** end the reach: it leaves a fully-permissioned role live in demo,
still trusted by the grove, and now unmanaged. **the door stays open and nobody watches it.** the
revoke that works is an `upsert` that rewrites the trust policy, walked at `[t3']`.

✅ **the limit binds the ROLE, never iam wholesale — three DAOs implement `set.delete`**
(`…RolePolicyAttachedInlineDao.ts:38`, `…RolePolicyAttachedManagedDao.ts:38`,
`…IamUserAccessKeyDao.ts:41`). so the role's **power** is declaratively deletable even where the role
is not: strip its attachments and the orphan left behind is **inert** rather than fully-permissioned.
⇒ F8 candidate ⑤, and the reason `[t5]`'s residual is smaller than it reads.

⚠️ **one honest qualifier on the speed of that, stated because an incident is exactly when it
matters:** the delete needs a demo-admin apply, and that login is **interactive SSO** — *"an agent
CANNOT do this step"* (`howto.reapply-demo-oidc-role`, and `case=3`'s `given`). so revocation is one
deleted statement **plus a human at a keyboard**. that is still far better than a key hunt across a
fleet, and it is not instant, and an incident runbook should say which. the next clone that tries the assume-role gets the same `AccessDenied`
as `case=2` — except now it is the correct answer, and the same off-target error text that was a
hazard in `case=2` is here a feature: the caller is genuinely no longer authorized.

🔴 **and here is what keeps that trap from a cut.** the provisioner does not
revoke by intuition — they revoke by a **plan they read first**, and the revoke has a **stated
expected shape**: apply 1 shows `DESTROY` on the attachments, apply 2 shows `UPDATE` on the role
alone. that one sentence is the whole fail-safe. a provisioner who reaches for the intuitive delete
gets a plan that **does not match what they were told to expect** — an absent row where two
`DESTROY`s were owed, or a `DESTROY` on the role where an `UPDATE` was owed — and they stop **before
they apply**. the silence `[t3]` calls unobservable is only unobservable while nobody has said what
should have been there.

⇒ it is not prevention: declastruct's behaviour lives in a dependency and the bad gesture stays
expressible. it is **detection, moved to the cheapest moment** — and a revoke is a deliberate,
plan-first act, so the check sits exactly where the actor's eyes already are. `case=3` already made a
plan-read an acceptance criterion for the *create* sense; the revoke sense needed it more.

**the second fail-safe is an order, and it is easy to mistake for a chore.** the two applies go
*strip the attachments, confirm, then upsert the trust statement* — and that sequence is not
tidiness. a holder with a live session can spend the ≤1h tail on an `iam:` write that repoints the
very statement the revoke is about to rewrite. strip first and their next call has no grant behind
it; upsert first and they are warned while still fully armed. **the order is the control.**

the provisioner also checks what is easy to forget in the relief of a completed revocation: **did
the shut of this door touch the other one?** `ehmpathy-demo-oidc` must read `KEEP` through the whole
revoke. it does, because the two roles were never entangled — which is `case=4`'s argument, finally
cashed at the moment it pays.

and one honest ending: **revocation ends future sessions; it does not undo the past.** if `case=5`
happened during the reach's life — a rogue process minted a role with a trust document of its own
choosing — that role is still there after the statement is deleted. the door is shut and something
may already be inside. this walk shows the shape of the residual rather than a pretence that
revocation is a rollback.

## the interaction this journey exposed — and no spotlight could

🔴 **`[t4]` and `case=2` `[t1]` are the same error.** an `AccessDenied` on this role means either
*"the reach is not wired yet"* or *"the reach was deliberately revoked"*, and **aws's message is
byte-identical in both cases.**

each individual demo was correct and neither could see this: `case=2` reads the error as a hazard to
teach past, `case=7` `[t4]` reads it as a success. only the walk that crosses both moments in one
sequence makes the collision visible.

⇒ **the design obligation:** the fix `case=2` already asks for — the role's `description` names the
paired repo and file — is now doing double duty, and must handle both readings. a description that
says only *"the caller half lives in ahbode/infrastructure"* will send a mid-revoke operator on a
hunt for a half that is absent **on purpose**.

**a better description states the current intent, never merely the topology** — e.g. it names the
reach as active and points at the paired file. then a revoked reach is a role whose description no
longer matches its trust policy, which is a readable signal instead of an ambiguous one. carried into
the execution stage as a concrete requirement rather than a nicety.

⚠️ **and the same reader-reach split `case=2` names applies here.** the role's `description` needs
`iam:GetRole` in demo, which the denied clone does not hold — so the intent statement is owed in
**both** places: the role description (for the provisioner and the auditor) **and** a note in
`provision/aws.auth/account=demo/` (for the clone, on disk, no aws call). a mid-revoke operator who
can only reach the repo must be able to tell "ended on purpose" from "not wired yet" **from the
repo**.

### 🔴 the two volunteered claims — real, and filed where they belong

neither is a fail-safe obligation. both are corrections this walk made to the yield's aha, and they
are kept because a corrected over-claim is worth more than a quiet retraction:

| the aha claimed | the walk found |
|---|---|
| the revoke is **immediate** | ⛔ **no.** `[t4']` — a trust-policy edit gates the next `AssumeRole` and does not reach a token already issued. a **≤1h tail**, writable under the ruled bundle. ⇒ this is not an unhandled edge; it is a **bounded window with a demonstrated control** (`[t4''']`) |
| the revoke is **one apply** | ⛔ **no.** declastruct applies in **declared array order** and the array must read **role first** (F5), so one plan would execute *upsert → strip* — the reverse of what `[t4''']` needs. ⚠️ and `applyChange` throws **per change**. ⇒ **two applies: strip, confirm, then upsert** — and the two-ness is the control, never a defect |

### 🔴 the two halves of "revoked", and why an incident needs both

`[t3']` and `[t4']` answer different questions, and the aha's phrase *"the reach ends"* runs them
together:

| the question | the answer | the act |
|---|---|---|
| *"can a NEW session be obtained?"* | ✅ **no**, at once | the trust-policy upsert — `[t3']` |
| *"can an EXTANT session still act?"* | ⛔ **yes**, for up to 1h | needs a **second** act — the `aws:TokenIssueTime` deny |

⚠️ **and the gap between them is exactly the window an adversary uses.** `case=5` `[t2']` is a
one-api-call re-entrenchment; the tail is an hour. so against a hostile holder, a revoke that does
only `[t3']` **loses the race by design**.

⇒ **the incident runbook owes BOTH steps, in this order** — the deny first (it stops the bleed), the
trust rewrite second (it stops re-entry). the reverse order hands the attacker the tail as warning.
